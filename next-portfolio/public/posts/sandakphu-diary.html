The Sandakphu Diary

It was the beginning of fall, or the end of the Durga puja season — what we call in Kolkata the quiet hangover after the festivals. All four of us were returning after an evening movie show when somebody said the sentence that starts most of our trips: "Shall we just do it this year?" This year, "it" was Sandakphu — the highest point of West Bengal, the place from which, on a clear morning, you can see four of the five highest mountains on Earth.

The plan fit on the back of a movie ticket: take the overnight train to New Jalpaiguri, a shared jeep up to Manebhanjan, and then walk. No porters booked, no elaborate itinerary. Just the Singalila Ridge, a pair of boots each, and whatever the weather decided to be.

The ridge road

The trail from Manebhanjan is an old Land Rover track that climbs steadily through villages where the morning smells of woodsmoke and steamed momos. We shared the path with loaded ponies, school children waving at strangers, and the occasional thundering Land Rover that has ferried trekkers up this ridge for decades.

By the second day the forest turned quiet — moss-heavy oaks and magnolias, the kind of forest where you hear your own footsteps. The teahouses along the way are basic in the best sense: a bunk, a blanket, endless pots of black tea, and hosts who treat your cold hands as a personal problem to be solved with more tea.

Sleeping Buddha

Somewhere near Kalipokhri we got our first proper view. The Kanchenjunga massif lines up on the horizon like a sleeping figure — the locals call it the Sleeping Buddha, and once you see it you cannot unsee it: the head, the folded arms, the blanket of glaciers.

We reached Sandakphu in the late afternoon, breathless and wind-battered, and cooked noodles in a trekker's hut while the clouds performed below us. At 3,636 metres, the air is thin enough to make you humble and clear enough to make you forget your knees.

The summit morning

The reason people drag themselves out of sleeping bags at 4 a.m. here is the sunrise. Everest, Lhotse, Makalu — and Kanchenjunga glowing closest of all. First light moves across the range like a slow torch being passed from peak to peak. We stood there mostly silent, passing around a thermos, taking fewer photos than we expected because some things simply refuse to fit in a frame.

"The mountains don't ask you to conquer them. They just ask you to show up." — written on the wall of a teahouse, author unknown

Coming down

The walk down to Sepi was long, dusty and full of that particular sadness of a trip ending. But we also came back with something: four friends, one ridge, and the shared agreement that the next "shall we just do it this year?" will be answered with far less resistance.

If you're planning your own Sandakphu trek, a few honest notes:

  • Best seasons are October–November (clear skies, post-monsoon green) and March–April (rhododendrons).
  • Carry cash — the teahouses don't take anything else, and the last working ATM is far behind you.
  • Layer up. Even in autumn, the summit night gets close to freezing.
  • Book the Singalila National Park permit at Manebhanjan; it takes minutes.

Until the next ridge.
